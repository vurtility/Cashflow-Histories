## 1. Research dossier

| # | Item | This episode |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The assumption** | Airlines make money flying people. The cheap airline that charges for everything is the one squeezing the most out of you, and the big legacy carriers are the dinosaurs. |
| 2 | **The truth** | In 2015 the cheap airline was more profitable than Delta. But the big carriers had quietly turned into credit-card businesses: Delta's payments from American Express ($8.2B in 2025) are bigger than its entire operating profit. Spirit's extras came out of its passengers' pockets; Delta's come out of a bank, funded by swipe fees that every shopper pays. |
| 3 | **Central question** | In 2015 Spirit was more profitable than Delta. Eleven years later Spirit doesn't exist and Delta made billions. How? |
| 4 | **Final payoff** | Spirit died because its only extra money came from its own passengers, and they were the people with the least to give. Delta survives because its extra money comes from everyone who pays with a card, flying or not. Delta didn't beat Spirit at flying. It stopped needing to win at flying. |
| 5 | **Shock bank** | ① Delta's American Express money ($8.2B, 2025) is bigger than its operating profit (~$5.8B) ② In 2020 the banks lent $25.8B against airline miles (United $6.8B, Delta $9B, American $10B), with the planes parked ③ Spirit's 2015 operating margin was 23.8%, against Delta's 16.2% ④ Spirit passengers paid more in fees ($68.57) than for the seat ($53.01) in 2023 ⑤ Delta got 73× as much from its card partner as Spirit did (2023) ⑥ Delta takes in about $22 million a day from Amex ⑦ United valued its miles programme at $21.9B ⑧ Without loyalty money, one analysis finds the big airlines' flying loses money ⑨ Fed research: about $15B a year flows from less savvy and poorer cardholders to richer ones ⑩ Baldanza, the man who built Spirit's fee model, died nine days before its first bankruptcy ⑪ Delta started as a crop-dusting company and went bankrupt in 2005 ⑫ Bastian: Delta card spend is close to 1% of US GDP |

| 7 | **Stakes / antagonist** | For Spirit: fuel, engine-grounded planes, a blocked merger, customers who can't absorb price rises. For Delta: the Credit Card Competition Act, which would cut the swipe fees the whole model runs on. |
| 8 | **Turning points** | 1996 (Delta's Amex card launches) · 2005 (Delta bankrupt; Baldanza takes over Spirit) · 2007 (Spirit goes ultra-low-cost) · 2015 (Spirit's record margin) · 2016 (Baldanza out) · 2020 (miles become collateral) · Jan 2024 (JetBlue deal blocked) · Nov 2024 (Spirit's first bankruptcy) · Aug 2025 (second) · 2 May 2026 (Spirit stops flying) |

`[BROLL: departures board, every Spirit flight "CANCELLED"]`

`[VISUAL: a cheque, amount blurred, "Pay to: Delta Air Lines"]`

### 1. HOME OF THE BARE FARE

`[BROLL: 1960s Michigan highway, trucks. Caption: "1964"]`
Spirit didn't start as an airline. It started in 1964 as a trucking company in Michigan, and spent decades as a small charter and holiday operator.

`[PHOTO: Ben Baldanza, face visible. Label "Ben Baldanza, CEO 2005–2016"]`
Then, in 2005, it hired Ben Baldanza. And Baldanza had an idea that sounded insane in America.

`[GFX: a fare ticket tearing into pieces: seat · bag · carry-on · seat choice · printed boarding pass]`
Sell the seat for almost nothing. Then charge for everything else. Your bag. Your carry-on. Choosing a seat. Even printing your boarding pass at the airport.

`[BROLL: Spirit ad. Caption: "Home of the Bare Fare"]`
By 2007, Spirit had become America's first true ultra-low-cost airline. It called itself the Home of the Bare Fare.

`[DOC: Consumerist headline, highlighted: "easily the most complained-about airline in the U.S."]`
Customers hated it. Spirit became the most complained-about airline in the country.

But here's what mattered: they kept buying.

`[GFX: Spirit operating margin rising to 23.8% in 2015, labelled "record"]`
By 2015, Spirit was making a record twenty-four cents of operating profit on every dollar. The airline everyone loved to hate was one of the most profitable in America.

`[GFX: Spirit 2023, one passenger: "Seat $53" · "Everything else $69"]`
And the model kept going. By 2023, the average Spirit passenger paid fifty-three dollars for the seat, and sixty-nine dollars for everything else.
◆ **P2 PLANTED**

`[VISUAL: line card]`
More for the extras than for the flight.

`[DOC: Jan 2016 headline: "Spirit Airlines CEO Baldanza replaced"]`
Baldanza himself was replaced in January 2016. By then, he'd built exactly what he set out to build.

`[SOT 10.2s: Sue Herrera, CNBC — "Baldanza transformed Spirit from a money-losing operation into an ultra low-cost carrier that charged for extra basic amenities, including seats that didn't recline." | CNBC Squawk Alley, 5 Jan 2016]`

So the cheap airline had cracked it. Rock-bottom fares, and the profit in the extras.

`[BROLL: Spirit plane taking off, slow motion]`
But the model had one weakness. And it was hiding in exactly where those extras came from.
◆ **L3 OPENED** · ◆ **CLIFFHANGER #1**

### 2. BANKRUPT IN ATLANTA

`[PHOTO: Huff Daland crop-duster biplane. Caption "Huff Daland Dusters, 1924"]`
Delta started even further from glamour: in 1924, as a crop-dusting company in Georgia.

`[DOC: 2005 headline: "Delta, Northwest file for bankruptcy"]`
And in September 2005, it went bankrupt, on the same day as Northwest.

`[BROLL: Delta and Northwest planes side by side]`
It came back, swallowed Northwest in 2008, and became one of the biggest airlines in the world.

So you'd assume Delta's comeback was about flying better: better planes, better service, better prices.

`[VISUAL: beat]`
Partly. But that's not where the most important money comes from.

`[LOGO: American Express beside Delta]`
In 1996, Delta started a co-branded credit card with American Express. It looked like a marketing deal. Earn miles when you shop, spend them on flights.

`[BROLL: American Express event, Palm Desert, Jan 2024]`
In January 2024, Delta's chief executive, Ed Bastian, was asked on CNBC what the relationship is worth.

`[SOT 6.1s: Ed Bastian, Delta CEO — "We will generate over $7 billion of revenue off of the card this year." | CNBC Squawk Box, 19 Jan 2024]`

`[GFX: Amex payments to Delta growing: $6.8B (2023) → $7.4B (2024) → $8.2B (2025)]`
He was right. And in 2025, it was eight point two billion.

`[GFX: two bars. Amex $8.2B vs Delta operating profit ~$5.8B. Small print: "Payments are revenue, not profit: Delta must honour the miles"]`
That's the payment from the cold open. Bigger than Delta's entire operating profit.
◆ **L2 CLOSED** — *payoff: the cheque is from a credit-card company*

`[GFX: "$8.2B ÷ 365 = $22.5M a day"]`
About twenty-two million dollars a day. From a company that doesn't fly a single plane.

`[VISUAL: a single "mile" token]`
Which raises a strange question. Why would a bank pay billions for airline miles?
◆ **L4 OPENED** · ◆ **CLIFFHANGER #2**

### 3. THE MOST VALUABLE THING DELTA SELLS

`[VISUAL: "Simplified example: round numbers, not Delta's actual rates"]`
Here's how it works, with simple numbers.

`[BROLL: supermarket checkout, card tapped on a terminal]`
Say you buy a hundred dollars of groceries on a Delta card. The supermarket pays a fee for taking your card. Call it a couple of dollars.

`[GFX: $100 → $2–3 fee to the card company → card company buys miles from Delta → miles land in your account]`
The card company uses part of that to buy miles from Delta, and puts them in your account.

`[GFX: Delta, coins in, no plane]`
Now look at what Delta just did. It got paid for a flight you haven't taken. You might take it next year. You might never take it at all.

`[BROLL: half-empty cabin]`
And when you do, it's often in a seat that would have flown empty anyway.

`[SOT 3.0s: Ed Bastian, Delta CEO — "You put those two things together, it's incredible magic that occurs." | CNBC Squawk Box, 19 Jan 2024]`

`[GFX: "Delta card spend ≈ 1% of US GDP" (Ed Bastian, CEO)]`
Bastian says spending on Delta's cards is now close to one percent of America's entire economy.

`[BROLL: shoppers paying with cash]`
And here's where it reaches your wallet, even if you've never flown Delta. Shops don't charge card users more. They raise prices for everyone.
◆ **P1 PAID OFF**

`[DOC: Federal Reserve paper, 2023, title and key line highlighted]`
Federal Reserve researchers estimate that rewards cards move about fifteen billion dollars a year from less savvy and poorer cardholders to richer, savvier ones.

`[QUOTE: card industry position, paraphrase]`
The card industry's answer is that rewards are funded by fees that shops choose to pay, because card customers spend more. That's the trade-off.

`[VISUAL: line card]`
Either way, the mile is the most valuable thing Delta sells.

`[BROLL: Delta plane parked, engines covered]`
But for years, nobody could say how valuable. Then, in 2020, every plane in America stopped flying, and the world found out.
◆ **L4 CLOSED** · ◆ **L5 OPENED** · ◆ **CLIFFHANGER #3**

### 4. COLLATERAL

`[BROLL: 2020, rows of parked airliners in the desert]`
Spring 2020. Passengers vanished almost overnight. The airlines were burning cash with nothing coming in.

They needed to borrow tens of billions of dollars. Normally, an airline borrows against its planes.

`[VISUAL: beat]`
Not this time.

`[DOC: United filing, highlighted: "MileagePlus… approximately $21.9 billion"]`
In June 2020, United valued its frequent-flyer programme at twenty-one point nine billion dollars, and borrowed six point eight billion against it.

`[DOC: Delta press release, Sept 2020: "SkyMiles financing… $9 billion"]`
In September, Delta borrowed nine billion against SkyMiles. The biggest debt deal the airline industry had ever seen.

`[DOC: American, March 2021: "AAdvantage… $10.0 billion"]`
American followed with ten billion against AAdvantage.

`[GFX: three stacks totalling "$25.8B, secured by miles"]`
Twenty-five point eight billion dollars, lent against miles. With the planes parked, the most valuable thing these airlines owned was a promise of free flights.

`[GFX: American's appraisal $19.5–31.5B vs its market value under $10B (2020). Small print: "Appraisals cited by the company; market value per Motley Fool"]`
Appraisals of American's programme came in at up to thirty-one billion dollars, at a time when the whole airline was worth less than ten.

`[GFX: one analysis: operating margins without loyalty revenue below zero for the big airlines. Small print: "Analyst estimate, 2025"]`
One 2025 analysis stripped the loyalty money out of the big airlines' results. Their flying, on its own, lost money.

`[VISUAL: line card]`
So Delta didn't survive by winning at flying. It built a business that no longer needs to.

`[BROLL: Spirit yellow plane]`
Which brings us back to Spirit. If the miles are this valuable, why didn't Spirit just do the same?
◆ **L5 CLOSED** · ◆ **L6 OPENED** · ◆ **CLIFFHANGER #4**

### 5. THE SQUEEZE

`[GFX: two bars, 2023 card money. Delta $6.8B vs Spirit $93M]`
It tried. Spirit had its own credit card too. In 2023 it brought in ninety-three million dollars.

Delta's brought in seventy-three times as much.

`[BROLL: Delta Sky Club lounge vs a Spirit gate area]`
Because a card like that only works if the people holding it spend a lot. Business travellers. Households putting everything on the card.

`[GFX: callback: Spirit passenger "Seat $53 · Everything else $69"]`
Spirit's customers were the opposite. They chose Spirit because every dollar mattered.
◆ **P2 PAID OFF**

So Spirit's extra money had only one place to come from: its own passengers. And they were the people with the least to give.
◆ **L3 CLOSED** — *payoff: the weakness was where the extras came from*

`[GFX: timeline: fuel up · planes grounded · fares down 2023]`
When costs rose, Delta's card money kept growing. Spirit had to squeeze its passengers harder. And price-sensitive passengers have options.

`[BROLL: JetBlue and Spirit planes]`
Spirit's way out was a merger. In May 2022, JetBlue offered to buy it. Spirit's chief executive went on CNBC to explain why the board said no.

`[SOT 9.4s: Ted Christie, Spirit CEO — "and then attempting to buy a competitor and take seats out of the market and raise fares. And that's that's going to be a big issue and one that our board viewed as insurmountable." | CNBC Squawk Box, 19 May 2022]`

`[DOC: July 2022 headline: "Spirit agrees to $3.8 billion JetBlue deal"]`
Two months later, Spirit said yes, for three point eight billion dollars.

`[DOC: Jan 2024 headline: "Judge blocks JetBlue–Spirit merger"]`
In January 2024, a federal judge blocked it. His reason: it would take seats out of the market and raise fares.

`[BROLL: Spirit premium seats, "Go Big" bundle]`
So Spirit tried the last thing it had: becoming Delta. It scrapped change fees and started selling premium bundles with bigger seats. The Home of the Bare Fare, selling comfort.

`[PHOTO: Ben Baldanza, face visible. Label "Ben Baldanza, 1961–2024"]`
On the ninth of November 2024, Ben Baldanza, the man who built Spirit's fee model, died. He was sixty-two.

`[DOC: 18 Nov 2024 headline: "Spirit Airlines files for bankruptcy"]`
Nine days later, Spirit filed for bankruptcy.

`[GFX: Spirit net losses 2020–2024 stacking to "$3.1B"]`
It had lost more than three billion dollars in five years. It came out of bankruptcy in March 2025, and filed again that August.

`[BROLL: news coverage of the shutdown]`
On the second of May 2026, a government rescue fell through, and Spirit stopped flying. Seventeen thousand jobs.

`[VISUAL: title card]`
So let's go back to the question. Spirit was more profitable than Delta. Why is one gone and the other thriving?

Delta has an answer, and it's eight billion dollars a year. But that answer has a weak spot too.
◆ **L7 OPENED** · ◆ **CLIFFHANGER #5**

### 6. THE THREAT

`[BROLL: US Capitol]`
Every card swipe pays a fee, and those fees fund the miles. So the biggest threat to Delta isn't another airline. It's Congress.

`[DOC: bill text: "Credit Card Competition Act"]`
A bill called the Credit Card Competition Act would push those fees down. It was reintroduced in January 2026.

`[QUOTE: Durbin and Marshall letter, 2023: "unfair, abusive and deceptive practices"]`
Its backers say airline loyalty programmes have turned into a way to make shoppers pay for frequent flyers.

`[QUOTE: Airlines for America, paraphrase: "would threaten the rewards programmes travellers value"]`
The airlines say it would gut the rewards millions of travellers count on. And they've fought it every time.

`[GFX: "Analysts: still a long shot"]`
For now, analysts call it a long shot. But Delta's single biggest source of profit depends on a fee that a vote in Washington could cut.

### VERDICT

`[VISUAL: title card]`
So why did Spirit die, and Delta didn't?

`[GFX: split screen: Spirit "money from passengers" · Delta "money from every card swipe"]`
Not because Delta flies better. Spirit's extra money came out of its passengers' pockets. Delta's comes out of everyone's.

`[BROLL: supermarket checkout again]`
Spirit charged you for your bag. Delta gets paid when you buy groceries.

`[GFX: callback: cold-open bars, Spirit 23.8% vs Delta 16.2%, then Spirit's bar fades]`
In 2015, Spirit had the better margin. But a margin is only as strong as the person paying it.

`[GFX: Delta target "$10B a year" from American Express]`
Delta now wants ten billion dollars a year from American Express. The more America swipes, the more Delta makes, whether anyone flies or not.

`[BROLL: Delta plane taking off at dusk]`
The question is what happens to an airline that makes more from a credit card than from flying, if Washington ever decides to cut the card fees.
