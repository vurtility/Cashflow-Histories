## 1. Company Man: The Decline of KFC…What Happened? (~12 min)

**Structure:** a warm, nostalgic opening, then a spoken table of contents ("the different owners… and the five biggest reasons"). Two lists are woven together: the owners in order (Colonel → 1964 investors for $2M → Heublein for $285M → R.J. Reynolds → Pepsi for ~$850M → Tricon/Yum) and five reasons (competition, menu, marketing, health, reputation). The ending adds a twist (China; most KFCs are outside the US) as an afterthought, then several comment prompts.

**Score:** first line 2 · paradox 3 · cliffhangers 1 · but/therefore 3 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 1 · answer beats guess 2 · loops closed 4 · derived numbers 1 · ending 2 · accuracy 3 · read-aloud 4 = **30**

**Why it works anyway:**
- **A nostalgia topic with a series formula.** "The Decline of X…What Happened?" applied to childhood brands (Chuck E. Cheese's 40×, Kmart 33×, Pizza Hut 27×, KFC 24×). The title *is* the question, the format is familiar, and viewers binge the series.
- **A host people trust.** First person, conversational, openly hedged ("what I believe", "I don't know"). It feels like a knowledgeable friend, not a lecture.
- **A dense bank of anecdotes.** The Colonel calling his own gravy "wallpaper paste"; KFC suing its own mascot; a fake Colonel getting into restricted areas at the UN; an Oprah coupon fiasco; the 30-year "mutant chicken" rumour. A memorable human moment roughly every minute.
- **Short:** about 12 minutes, so the per-minute bar is low.
- **A price ladder through the owners** ($2M → $285M → $850M) gives an implicit sense of progress without any unit economics.

**Weaknesses:**
- The five reasons are what a viewer would guess.
- No reveal, no cliffhangers, no per-unit numbers.
- **The best fact is buried.** "KFC is shrinking in America but over 80% of its restaurants are abroad" is a paradox that could carry the whole video, and it's used as a closing caveat.

**Accuracy (medium confidence on the flags):**
- These match what I know: 3,400 stores by 1970, about twice McDonald's; the Heublein 1971, R.J. Reynolds 1982 and Pepsi 1986 sales; Tricon 1997; Chick-fil-A overtaking KFC in US sales in the early 2010s; the Colonel's "wallpaper paste" criticism; "over 80% of locations abroad".
- Worth checking: whether KFC's lawsuit against the Colonel was "unsuccessful" and its details; the "Grilled Nation" UN stunt; Darrell Hammond "feeling misled".

**Same topic, two channels:** Modern MBA's KFC video got ~3.5M views and this one got ~3.9M, with completely different approaches (numbers-led, ~30 min vs host-led anecdotes, ~12 min). That's more evidence that the topic does most of the work on views.

**Lessons to fold into the skill:**
1. Topic scoring: add **nostalgia / "what happened to X?"**, meaning brands from viewers' childhoods that faded. A repeatable series title format multiplies this.
2. Research: keep an **anecdote bank** (memorable human moments, quotes, stunts, rumours) alongside the shock bank. Aim for one about every 60–90 seconds.
3. Ownership or price ladders ($2M → $285M → $850M) as a light structure device for "rise and fall" stories.
4. **Don't bury the paradox.** If the best fact reframes the whole story ("shrinking at home, huge abroad"), it belongs in the cold open or at the midpoint, not in a caveat.
5. Voice: an optional first-person host persona with an honest opinion. For a faceless channel, use a light touch: occasional "here's what I think" at verdict moments.

**Opening rewritten our way** (using the video's own claims, unchecked):
> In 1970 there were twice as many KFCs in America as McDonald's. Today KFC has been closing American restaurants for years, and the chain that invented fast-food chicken has been overtaken by one that closes on Sundays. So KFC is dying, right? Not quite. More than four out of every five KFCs on Earth are now outside the United States. So what happened to KFC in America, and why did the same company thrive everywhere else?

## 2. MagnatesMedia: How WhatsApp Makes Money: The INSANE Story of WhatsApp (~30 min)

**Structure:** chapters headed by year, each acting as a cliffhanger: 2009 Birth → 2010 The Money Problem → 2011 The Business Model → 2014 The Acquisition → 2016 Free? → 2017 #deletefacebook → Present Day → (sponsor) → In Defence of WhatsApp & Facebook. The opening line comes back at the end as a bookend.

**Score:** first line 5 · paradox 4 · cliffhangers 5 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 4 · answer beats guess 3 · loops closed 5 · derived numbers 2 · ending 5 · accuracy 2 · read-aloud 4 = **47** (tied with Samsung for the highest)

**What it does well:**
- **The opening:** an aphorism plus an accusation ("If you're not paying for the product, you are the product. You are being programmed."). Then an **investigator framing**: "What started out as a very simple business question led me down a rabbit hole much darker than I would have imagined, that affects every single one of us." An innocent question leads to a dark answer, with personal stakes for the viewer.
- **Every chapter ends on a hook:** "but strangely, that didn't matter"; "unless of course you just sell the app for a huge payout"; "so what could go wrong?"; "so there's nothing to worry about, right? …Right."; "it's now time for a pretty major plot twist".
- **A character arc:** Brian Acton is turned down for a job at Facebook → builds WhatsApp → Facebook buys it for $19B → he quits, walking away from ~$850M → puts $50M into Signal: "the David going against the Goliath I created." The script explicitly zooms out to show the arc.
- **The big number gets a paradox and comparisons:** $19B for an app with tiny revenue, compared with Instagram's $1B price, 20 Mars missions and 13 Burj Khalifas.
- **It answers the viewer's objection before they raise it:** "Are you not getting a bit carried away here? Zuckerberg said it would stay independent… right?"
- **A fairness section as the final twist:** "In Defence of WhatsApp & Facebook" admits the video's own ad funding and presents the trade-off. Fairness is used as a plot twist, not a footnote.
- **It explicitly returns to the title question** ("Let's again return to the original question…") and recaps the answer: the $1 fee → investors → the payout → data → WhatsApp Business → ads.
- **Archival voices** (Zuckerberg's apology montage, Acton, a senator) add documentary texture.

**Weaknesses:**
- **The title's literal question is never answered with numbers.** There are no WhatsApp Business revenue figures, no per-user value, and "it seems evident it's not making a profit" is a guess.
- **Missing derived number:** $19B ÷ WhatsApp's users at the time of the acquisition = roughly $40 per user (check the user count). That's the obvious per-unit shock and it isn't used.
- **The answer is the one the viewer already expects** ("they want your data"), and the opening aphorism gives it away. The defence section rescues it partly.
- It's long (30 minutes), with a mid-roll sponsor plus a plug for the creator's own course placed at the "plot twist" cliffhanger.

**Accuracy (medium confidence on the flags):**
- These match what I know: the $19B price; Acton leaving in 2017 and forfeiting ~$850M; $50M into Signal; Koum leaving in 2018; the EU's €110M fine in 2017 for misleading statements in the merger review; the Zuckerberg Harvard messages; Telegram not encrypting end-to-end by default.
- **"WhatsApp had actually been sharing user information with Facebook for years already" is probably overstated.** As I understand it, the EU's finding was that Facebook wrongly claimed automatic account matching was impossible in 2014. Data sharing came in with the 2016 policy update.
- **"$1.2M revenue in the nine months before the deal"** should be checked against Facebook's 2014 SEC merger filing. My recollection is that it reported 2013 revenue of about $10M with a large loss.
- **"WhatsApp charges businesses for late replies" is garbled.** The Business API charged for messages sent outside the 24-hour customer-service window.
- **Speculation presented as fact:** "some people believe the encryption is not as secure" and "I think it's already there", with no named sources.

**Lessons to fold into the skill:**
1. **The investigator framing:** "a simple question that led somewhere darker". The innocent question becomes the title, and the answer escalates.
2. **Personal stakes:** connect the business to the viewer's own phone, wallet or data ("this affects every one of us").
3. **Chapter titles as cliffhangers** ("2016: Free?"), also used as YouTube chapters.
4. **A character arc** (rejected → creator → betrayed → rival) and the "zoom out on the arc" moment.
5. **Answer the viewer's objection** ("You might be thinking… right? …Right.").
6. **Fairness as a plot twist:** the steelman can be a whole late section, not a footnote.
7. **Explicitly return to the title question** before the ending and recap the answer in one breath.
8. **Make huge numbers tangible:** prefer a per-unit figure ($ per user) first, and use everyday comparisons second.
9. **Warning:** insinuation and unnamed "some believe" claims cost credibility. A dark tone needs sourced facts.

## 3. Business Casual: The Rise of Nike: How One Man Built a Billion-Dollar Brand (~8 min)

**Structure:** a generic summary opening ("the world's biggest sportswear company… worth over $100 billion… how that magic happened"), then a sponsor at 0:23. A tight origin story: Knight's Stanford paper (Japanese cameras beat German ones, so could Japanese shoes beat Adidas?) → flies to Japan and invents a company name on the spot → 12 pairs sold from his car → Bowerman partnership, $500 each → 300 pairs at $3.33 → sales of $8K → $20K → $300K → Onitsuka's supply squeeze → "they held all the cards" → the Nike name, the $35 swoosh → "from then on, the story of Nike became one of growth" → sponsor, "consider subscribing".

**Score:** first line 2 · paradox 2 · cliffhangers 2 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 3 · midpoint reveal 3 · answer beats guess 3 · loops closed 3 · derived numbers 2 · ending 1 · accuracy 4 · read-aloud 4 = **33**

**What works:**
- **A moment of audacity:** making up a company name in a meeting with the Japanese founder and walking out as the exclusive US distributor ("he went ahead and just did it").
- **A good plant and payoff:** Bowerman cutting open every shipment and sending design changes to Japan ("effectively designing Onitsuka's shoes for them"), later revealed as the escape route ("the Cortez was Bowerman's design… they held all the cards").
- **Revenue as a ladder of numbers:** $8K → $20K → $300K. An analogy from his student paper (cameras → shoes). The iconic $35 swoosh.
- **Short and clean.** This channel's outliers are all founder and empire stories (Rockefeller 9.7×, J.P. Morgan, the Rothschilds, Lamborghini, Carnegie, Nike).

**Weaknesses:**
- A generic opening and an early sponsor.
- **The real conflict is skipped.** The break with Onitsuka ended in a lawsuit. "He started importing before his contract expired" hints at it, then the script moves on.
- **The last act collapses** to "from then on… growth".
- The title says "one man", while the script itself shows Bowerman did the innovating.

**Accuracy:** good. The $500 each, $3.33 per pair, $8K first year, Davidson's $35 swoosh, Jeff Johnson coming up with "Nike", and Bowerman's 1966 jogging book all match what I know. "Contract would end in 1972" simplifies a messier, litigated split.

**Lessons:**
1. Founder origin stories of mega-brands work best with an **audacity moment** and a **planted escape route**.
2. Never skip the conflict scene. That's where the cliffhanger lives.
3. **Don't rush the final act** into "and then they grew". End on a reframe instead.

## 4. How Money Works: The (Overdue) Collapse of Short Term Rentals (~13.5 min)

**Structure:** a stakes-heavy opening claim ("a $100 billion market that reshaped global tourism, accelerated a housing crisis… became everything wrong with modern real estate"), then a **montage of news clips** (party ban, angry host, a debt allegation, a politician). A quick origin, a contrast ("worth more than Hilton and Wyndham combined"), then "four parties… four unique problems" (hosts, customers, the public, the platform) + a bonus fifth. The channel's tagline comes about 5:50 in ("so it's time to learn how money works"). It closes with cross-promotion and a value-based newsletter call to action ("to keep on learning how money works").

**Score:** first line 4 · paradox 3 · cliffhangers 2 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 3 · answer beats guess 3 · loops closed 4 · derived numbers 3 · ending 2 · accuracy 2 · read-aloud 4 = **38**

**What works:**
- **A thesis with attitude.** "The people who it's hardest to feel sorry for: the hosts"; "unless they were really stupid". The opinions have an edge, and the voice is distinctive.
- **A real mechanism in each section.** Hosts: short-term lets earned up to double the long-term rent even at half occupancy. That extra income qualified owners for more mortgages, leading to oversaturation, a narrowing gap and forced sales. Platform: a 3% host fee plus ~14% guest fee is a ~17% cut, against Uber's few dollars per ride, so Airbnb is profitable but has no moat beyond its brand.
- **A plant and payoff.** "Airbnb still spends billions promoting [the stay-with-a-local-host image] for a really important reason that will come up later" (7:25). Paid off at 11:07: shared stays are harder to regulate, so the folksy image is a political shield. That's a genuine hidden-economics insight.
- **The stakeholder structure.** Mapping every party (customer, host, public, platform) and each one's problem is a reusable format.
- **A news-clip montage opening** signals "the world is fighting about this" in about 15 seconds.
- **A signature line for the channel** ("time to learn how money works").

**Weaknesses:**
- **The title overclaims.** "Collapse", while the script itself says Airbnb is profitable. The thesis outruns the evidence.
- A list structure (4 problems + a bonus), no single reveal, and a weak cross-promo ending.

**Accuracy (medium confidence):**
- **"Bookings have only increased 70% since 2019, gross booking value has increased 67%"** contradicts its own point (in the transcript as given). The figures are wrong or garbled.
- **"Mill University"** is presumably McGill.
- **The fines range is written backwards:** "between $56.8 million and $32.2 million".
- **A news clip alleging hidden Airbnb debt "possibly a billion dollars"** is used as evidence with no verification.
- The split-fee structure (~3% host, ~14% guest) matches what I know.

**Lessons:**
1. **The stakeholder-squeeze format:** every party in the market and the problem each now has. It's a good alternative to the tournament format for marketplaces and platforms.
2. **An economic mechanism in every section** (yield maths, leverage loops), not just a fact.
3. **Opinions with an edge** make a distinctive voice. Keep them clearly labelled as opinion.
4. **The news-clip montage cold open.** For a faceless channel, use a rapid montage of on-screen headline cards (quoted, sourced).
5. **A channel signature line** said once per episode after the hook can build the brand.
6. **Don't let the title's thesis outrun the evidence.** A "collapse" that isn't happening costs credibility with exactly the viewers who'd subscribe.

## 5. Logically Answered: When Exploiting Customers Becomes Unprofitable: Sprint's Painful Death (~21 min)

**Structure:** a cold open with a **foil**. Sprint had 55M customers and lost billions, then was "bailed out by T-Mobile, who themselves were fighting against bankruptcy… T-Mobile staged one of the biggest corporate comebacks in history; Sprint would be completely forgotten. Sprint had a bigger brand, more customers and much more money. How did Sprint die?" Chapters: Bad Bets (the Nextel merger of equals) → Tension (the iPhone, Palm Pre, WiMAX) → Even Worse Bets (a $20B iPhone commitment, the rip-and-replace rollout without fibre) → A Bad Lifeline (SoftBank, Masayoshi Son's failed T-Mobile pursuit, cost-cutting) → the 2020 merger. The verdict: it was decisions, not the network. The end card links to the T-Mobile twin video.

**Score:** first line 3 · paradox 5 · cliffhangers 4 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 3 · answer beats guess 3 · loops closed 5 · derived numbers 2 · ending 4 · accuracy 4 · read-aloud 4 = **45**

**What works:**
- **The twin/foil paradox.** Two companies in the same hole, one with more of everything, and opposite outcomes. The question holds itself for 20 minutes, and the ending answers it directly by comparing the two.
- **Interruptions with attitude:** "So what did Sprint do? They fired them. That's right, fired." (1,000 customers) "How generous!"
- **A rule-of-three joke:** "AT&T offered the iPhone. Verizon offered the Droid. Sprint offered the… Palm Pre?"
- **Push and pull on good news.** The customer service turnaround is a real "silver lining", then it's undercut: "'Most improved' doesn't always mean 'great'… compare anything to a dumpster fire."
- **Scenes built from sourced insider quotes:** Son's meeting with the FCC chairman ("You could see Masa digging himself into a hole"; "It never occurred to us that he would not listen").
- **Contrasting numbers:** $1.3B of network investment against rivals' $6–10B; the stock falling from $53 to $11; losing $3.3B a year.
- **A paired video** (Sprint / T-Mobile) creates a series hook at the end.

**Weaknesses:**
- The title's "exploiting customers" theme fades after the first act.
- The verdict ("leadership and short-termism") is fairly generic.
- Few per-unit numbers (churn, revenue per user, network spend per subscriber).

**Accuracy (medium confidence):** these match what I know: the 2005 Nextel merger, the ~1,000 customers "fired" in 2007, the Palm Pre, WiMAX/Clearwire, the reported $20B/4-year iPhone commitment, SoftBank's $21.6B deal in 2013, Son abandoning T-Mobile in Aug 2014, Claure as CEO, the $26B merger in 2020, and Judge Marrero's ruling. **Check:** "stock $53 in 2005" (that looks high to me); "a 5-year iPhone exclusivity" (Verizon got the iPhone in early 2011).

**Lessons:**
1. **The twin/foil format:** pair the subject with a rival that faced the same problem and chose differently. The ending answers itself.
2. **The blunt-answer interrupt** ("So what did they do? They fired them.") and **rule-of-three comic juxtapositions**.
3. **Undercut good news** to keep tension ("most improved isn't great").
4. **Build scenes from sourced insider quotes** (WSJ, Bloomberg and court reporting).
5. **Paired episodes** (the loser and the winner) are a natural series.

## 6. Wall Street Millennial: Luckin Coffee Fraud Explained (~9 min)

**Structure:** "What's up guys" plus a generic macro primer (Chinese IPOs, accounting standards), then a straight timeline:
- founded 2017 with an app-only, "AI-picked locations" pitch
- $200M raised, aggressive free-drink discounts
- 2,500 stores by Jan 2019
- a NASDAQ IPO, the fastest to ~$3B since the dot-com era
- a $50 peak and ~$9B valuation
- **"However, not everyone was buying."** Muddy Waters publishes an anonymous 89-page report: hundreds of people filmed 10,000+ hours of store footage, and sales were inflated by up to 88%, ad spend by 150%
- denial, and Citron publicly backs Luckin
- the April 2020 confession of $310M in faked sales; the stock falls 80%
- the free-drink rush; fines; Chapter 15; the rebound off its lows; the US Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act
- "like, subscribe and share".

**Score:** first line 1 · paradox 3 · cliffhangers 3 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 3 · midpoint reveal 4 · answer beats guess 4 · loops closed 3 · derived numbers 3 · ending 1 · accuracy 4 · read-aloud 3 = **36**

**What works:**
- **The fraud arc is naturally a thriller:** a dazzling rise → a doubter → denial → a credible ally defends them → confession → aftermath.
- **The investigator's method is the best detail:** 10,000 hours of covert store footage to count customers and items per order. Viewers love "how they caught them".
- **A counterintuitive mini-question:** "Why would a company inflate its *expenses*?" Answered: to explain where the cash was going.
- **Doubt as push and pull:** a respected short seller goes long and calls it "not a fraud", so the viewer wavers right before the confession.
- **An ironic aftermath with a real quote:** app downloads jump from 45K to 325K a day as people rush to claim free drinks before the company dies ("you don't know how much longer Luckin can hold on").

**Weaknesses:**
- The weakest opening of all ten scripts ("what's up guys" plus a macro primer).
- A generic like/subscribe/share ending.
- **The most interesting open question is handed to the comments:** "why did the stock rally 800%?"
- **It's dated.** What happened next is the biggest twist of all. Luckin recovered and went on to overtake Starbucks in China on store count and revenue (around 2023). Any version made now must end there.

**Accuracy (medium confidence):** these match what I know: the Muddy Waters report on 31 Jan 2020, the ~$310M (RMB 2.2bn) of faked 2019 sales, the CEO and COO fired, the SEC's $180M settlement, a ~$9M fine in China, Chapter 15 in Feb 2021, and the HFCAA. **Check:** the IPO valuation stated as $2.9B (my recollection is nearer $4B at the $17 IPO price).

**Lessons:**
1. **Treat fraud and exposé stories as a power-story variant** with a fixed six-beat arc (rise → doubter → denial → defender → confession → aftermath).
2. **Show the investigator's method** and turn it into numbers (footage hours, the inflation %).
3. **Use counterintuitive mini-questions** ("why inflate expenses?").
4. **Always check what happened next.** The aftermath can be bigger than the story, and it is the ending.
5. **Topic idea for our channel:** *"The Coffee Chain That Faked Its Sales… Then Actually Beat Starbucks"* (Luckin). Reach is high (Starbucks), there's a huge gap between assumption and truth, it has a contrarian title, and data is public (SEC filings, results releases). Verify the comeback figures before pitching it.

## 7. Company Man: The Decline of Chuck E. Cheese's…What Happened? (~11.5 min)

**Structure:** the host opens personally ("my audience keeps me up to date on bankruptcy filings… I used to love this place"). Then quick business facts: 56% of sales from games and merchandise vs 44% food, 16% from birthday packages, 400 seats, 75 games. A nostalgia beat (the redesigned mascot). The bankruptcy is introduced with a **promise** ("I'll talk more about it later, I have all these graphs"). Then the history:
- Atari founder Nolan Bushnell noticed that a $1,500 arcade machine earned its operator $30–50K over its life, so he decided to be the operator. Pizza takes time to cook, so customers play while they wait.
- The coyote costume that arrived as a rat ("Rick Rat's Pizza").
- He bought the concept back from Warner for $500K.
- The rival ShowBiz was born from a broken franchise deal; an expansion race; the 1983 video-game crash; bankruptcy in 1984.
- **The imitator buys the original** ("like Lucky Charms being acquired by Marshmallow Mateys").
- Then the present: revitalisation efforts and rising costs, then "honestly, I would never expect these factors to lead to a bankruptcy… **so here's the real issue**": the 2014 Apollo buyout ($950M) tripled the debt to over $1B, plus the Peter Piper deal, so interest turned operating profit into losses in 5 of 6 years.
- The verdict as a metaphor: "a person living so far beyond their means that a temporary pay cut led to the bank foreclosing on their house." Then comment prompts.

**Score:** first line 2 · paradox 3 · cliffhangers 3 · but/therefore 4 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 4 · answer beats guess 4 · loops closed 4 · derived numbers 3 · ending 4 · accuracy 4 · read-aloud 3 = **42**

**Why ~40×:**
- **A news peg.** It came out right after the June 2020 bankruptcy filing, so search and news demand were at a peak.
- Nostalgia for a childhood brand.
- **A real reveal that beats the obvious guess** (the pandemic, kids on phones): private-equity debt.

**Lessons:**
1. **Timeliness is a topic factor.** Publish while the company is in the news (a bankruptcy, scandal, merger or earnings shock). Keep a list of evergreen scripts ready to adapt when news breaks.
2. **Withhold the real cause.** Walk through the obvious explanations, concede they don't add up ("still making tens of millions a year"), then: "here's the real issue."
3. **Everyday analogies for finance** (a household living beyond its means; a copycat cereal buying the original brand).
4. **An origin story with a business-model insight** ($1,500 machine → $30–50K for the operator).
5. Promise the graphs and numbers early ("I'll show you later") as a plant.

**Accuracy:** good. Bushnell/Atari, Pizza Time Theatre in 1977, the $500K buyback from Warner, ShowBiz/Brock, the 1984 bankruptcy, the 2014 Apollo deal (~$950M in equity), Peter Piper Pizza and the 2020 filing all match what I know.

## 8. Asianometry: The Tragedy of Compaq (~33 min)

**Structure:** a **staccato number ladder** to open: "Founded in 1982. First year revenue? $111 million. Zero to $111 million. One year." Then year 2 (+200%), the Fortune 500 in year 3, $1.2B fastest ever. "That is when the problems began. **The tragedy of Compaq is that they led the revolution. And then, as so often happens, the revolution turned on them.**" The chapters:
- Growth engines (product, speed, distribution), with an explicit plant: the dealer-only channel "also eventually became their fatal weakness."
- The high (hubris: Harvard Business Review praising that "cost ranks relatively low").
- Attack of the clones (modularity: in the end only Microsoft's software and Intel's chips mattered; Dell/Gateway priced 67% below).
- The irony punch: "You can charge a premium price if you build the best machines." "A few months later, Swavely retired."
- **The boardroom scene:** a 15-hour board meeting; the chairman secretly sent two staff to a trade show, who built two PCs in a motel room in three days; "Yes you can." The founding CEO is fired.
- The turnaround (price war, Presario, #1 worldwide), then hubris again (a $50B target, buying DEC for $9.6B).
- **The plant pays off:** the dealer channel blocks direct sales against Dell.
- The HP merger ("taking two stones and tying them together to see if they float").
- A reflective conclusion (commoditisation; Intel and Microsoft "lorded over a fiefdom").

**Score:** first line 5 · paradox 4 · cliffhangers 3 · but/therefore 5 · re-hooks 4 · midpoint reveal 5 · answer beats guess 4 · loops closed 5 · derived numbers 3 · ending 3 · accuracy 4 · read-aloud 4 = **49** (the highest of all twelve)

**What makes it the best-crafted script:**
- **A tragedy thesis:** the strength becomes the fatal weakness, planted explicitly and paid off 18 minutes later.
- **A double fall:** rise → fall → turnaround → hubris → fall again. Two arcs in one video.
- **Hubris → irony punches,** made from the company's own confident quotes.
- **A scene that shows rather than tells:** the motel-room demo PCs. A boardroom confrontation as the climax, at the midpoint.
- **A mechanism explained:** modularity commoditises the box.
- **Strong sources:** memoirs (Canion's *Open*), Computer History Museum oral histories, HBR interviews and contemporary press quotes.

**Weaknesses:**
- 33 minutes long, and the HP section is diffuse.
- The ending is reflective rather than punchy.
- Few cliffhangers between chapters.
- A niche audience (tech history), so lower reach despite the craft: about 10× its channel median but 1.6M views.

**Accuracy:** good and well sourced. One minor point: Compaq became the world's #1 PC maker around 1994, not 1995 as implied.

**Lessons:**
1. **The staccato number-ladder cold open** for growth stories.
2. **The tragedy/irony format:** name the strength that will kill them, early and explicitly.
3. **Irony punches from the company's own confident quotes.**
4. **Build the climax as a scene** with a concrete demonstration.
5. **Mine memoirs, oral histories, HBR or press interviews and court records** for primary quotes.
