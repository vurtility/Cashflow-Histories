# Script framework: full craft rules

The detailed rules behind SKILL.md. Stage numbers here match the stages in SKILL.md (this file has no Stage 5; fact-checking lives in SKILL.md).

The goal: the viewer always has **at least one question they need answered**, keeps **learning something they didn't expect**, and every question we raise **gets a real payoff**.

Work through the four stages in order. Don't start writing lines until Stages 1 and 2 are done.

---

## STAGE 1 — Research dossier (before any writing)

Fill all of this in. If a box is weak, research more. Don't start writing until every box is strong.

| # | Item | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The assumption** | What the average viewer believes about this company. *"The hotel giants must be terrified of Airbnb."* |
| 2 | **The truth** | The surprising reality that overturns or deepens it. *"The hotel giants already are Airbnb. Hilton owns under 2% of its rooms and makes its money from fees."* |
| 3 | **The central question** | One question the whole video answers. Phrase it as a paradox. *"How does an airline make billions selling flights below cost?"* |
| 4 | **The final payoff** | The answer, expressed as an insight, not a list. It must be *better* than what the viewer would guess. |
| 5 | **Shock bank** (10+) | Facts that make someone say "wait, what?". Rank them by strength. Each one needs a source. |
| 6 | **Derived numbers** | Turn annual-report totals into per-unit maths a viewer can feel: per passenger, per day, per second, per employee, versus a rival. **These are usually the strongest shocks, because nobody else has worked them out.** |
| 7 | **Stakes / antagonist** | What could break this company? Who wants it dead? A rival, a regulator, a supplier, physics? |
| 8 | **Turning points** | 3–5 moments where the story could have gone the other way. |
| 9 | **The human** | One person whose decisions drive the story (founder, CEO, rival). |
| 10 | **Unresolved future** | The live question that isn't settled yet. This becomes the ending tension. |

### Fact rules
- Every number has a source, and the source goes in the description.
- Derived numbers show their working in the research notes, e.g. *$1bn of debt ÷ ~$50m a year of operating income = 20 years of every dollar of profit, before any interest.*
- Flag company-claimed figures as company-claimed, on screen.
- No invented quotes. Paraphrase on screen and label it a paraphrase.

## STAGE 2 — Structure: the question ladder

Each act **answers the loop the previous act opened, then opens a bigger one**. Never close a loop without opening another.

Between beats, use **"BUT" or "THEREFORE", never "AND THEN"**. If two beats join with "and then", the viewer has no reason to keep watching. Find the tension or cut the beat.

```
COLD OPEN ─ paradox + stakes + promise         opens L1 (central question)
   │
ACT 1 ─ "The setup"                            opens L2, teases the answer to L1
   │     ends on CLIFFHANGER #1
ACT 2 ─ "The obvious answer" (and why it's     closes L2, PARTIALLY answers L1
   │     not enough)                            opens L3: "but that doesn't explain…"
   │     ends on CLIFFHANGER #2
ACT 3 ─ "The real answer" — MIDPOINT REVEAL    closes L1 with the big payoff
   │     (the strongest shock in the bank)      opens L4: "and that's exactly what makes it vulnerable"
ACT 4 ─ "The threat"                           raises the stakes
   │     ends on CLIFFHANGER #3
ACT 5 ─ "The verdict"                          closes L3/L4, lands the final insight
   │
OUTRO ─ callback to the cold open image, with new meaning
```

### Rules for each section

**Cold open (0:00–1:00)**
- First line: a **specific, surprising fact or image**, not context. No "In 1985…", no "Today we're looking at…".
- Within 20 seconds: state the **paradox** (two facts that shouldn't both be true).
- Within 45 seconds: **promise** what they'll understand by the end, and hint that the answer is **not what they think**.
- **Do NOT reveal the answer.** The cold open sets up the question; it never answers it.
- Optional: **plant** one detail that only makes sense later.

**Act transitions = cliffhangers.** The last line of every act should be one of these:
- An unanswered question: *"So what did Sprint do with its unhappiest customers?"*
- A twist reversal: *"It worked. And it nearly destroyed them."*
- A teased number: *"There's one number in Chuck E. Cheese's accounts that explains the bankruptcy, and it isn't the pandemic."*
- A stakes jump: *"Then, in 1983, the video-game market crashed."*

**The answer arrives as a reveal, not a list.** If the payoff is "4 reasons why", that's a listicle. Turn it into: *obvious reasons → "but that's still not enough" → the hidden reason*. Save the best item for last and set it up.

**Re-hook every 60–90 seconds.** In each window, include at least one of:
- A new shock fact
- A loop opened or closed
- A "you" moment (put the viewer in the scene: *"When you buy a smartphone in South Korea, you buy it from Samsung."*)
- A pattern interrupt (change of tone, a single-word line, a rhetorical question, a sudden visual)

**The ending must pay off, not summarise.** The last 60 seconds should *reframe* the cold open: the same image or number means something new now. Close with the unresolved future question (Stage 1, item 10) for lingering tension, then the subscribe line.

**The subscribe line (the only call to action, always last):** name the specific thing the viewer learned or now sees differently, then make subscribing the logical way to keep getting that. *"If you'll never look at a bucket of KFC the same way again, that's what this channel does: the economics nobody explains, one company at a time. Subscribe and you'll get the next one."* No sponsor reads, no begging, no like or bell requests, no mid-video asks.

## STAGE 3 — Line-level craft

| Device | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | "Ten thousand hours of covert store footage." | "Extensive research." |
| **Make numbers tangible** | "A $1,500 arcade machine earned its owner $30,000 to $50,000." | "Arcade games were very profitable." |
| **Contrast** | Put two numbers side by side and let the gap shock. | A lone number with nothing to compare it to. |
| **Withholding** | "Airbnb keeps advertising stays with a local host for a really important reason. It comes up later." *(paid off minutes later)* | Explaining immediately. |
| **Foreshadowing** | "Remember that number. It comes back." | Surprises with no setup (they feel random). |
| **Earned reveals** | Set up the expectation, then break it. | A reveal with no expectation in place. |
| **Rhythm** | Short. Short. Then a longer line that lands the point. | Every sentence the same length. |
| **Voice** | Confident, slightly wry, never sneering. | Hedging ("it could be argued that…"). |
| **Cut the throat-clearing** | Start each section on the interesting thing. | "Now let's talk about…" |
| **Worked hypothetical** | "Say there are two companies, each worth $50m, and the family owns half of one…", then apply it to the real case. Labelled on screen as simplified. | Describing a mechanism in abstract terms ("through complex cross-shareholdings…"). |
| **Chain reveal** | "…the pension fund. Which was run by the ministry. Which was run by the government. Which was run by the President." | Stating the conclusion first ("the President influenced the vote"). |
| **Interrupt cliffhanger** | Set up a hypothetical risk, then cut to the real event: "In May 2014, this happened." | Narrating events in order with no setup. |
| **Close story threads** | Every "this would be a death blow" gets its "here's what actually happened". | Leaving a threat hanging because the main question has been answered. |

**Write for the ear.** Read every line aloud. If you'd stumble saying it, rewrite it. Write numbers the way they're spoken in the voiceover script ("nine point six billion"), and use figures in the on-screen graphics ("$9.6bn").

**Fairness rule:** when a beat criticises the company, state the company's own stated reason first, then make the point. It reads as more credible and adds push and pull.

## Device toolkit (from 12 audited high performers)

Pick deliberately; don't use everything. Each device names its source, so you can look up the full example in `audit-log.md`.

### Openings (first 30–60 seconds)
| Device | Example | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Paradox as the first line** | "Geographically speaking, South Korea should be poor." Then stack the evidence and answer "…but it's not". | A clear contradiction exists. |
| **Staccato number ladder** | "Founded in 1982. First year revenue? $111 million. Zero to $111 million. One year." | Growth stories, rise-and-fall stories. |
| **Investigator framing** | "What started out as a very simple business question led me down a rabbit hole much darker than I would have imagined." | The answer gets darker or bigger than the question. |
| **Aphorism + accusation** | "If you're not paying for the product, you are the product." Bookend it at the end. | Consumer apps, "free" products. |
| **Foil paradox** | "Sprint had a bigger brand, more customers and much more money. How did Sprint die?" | The twin/foil format. |
| **Headline montage** | 4–6 rapid quoted headlines or news cards showing that the world is fighting about this. | Controversial, timely topics. |
| **Personal stakes** | "…that affects every single one of us." / "It's the app on your phone right now." | Consumer data, prices, housing. |

Avoid: industry primers, "What's up guys", spoken tables of contents, sponsor reads.

### Holding tension
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Chapter titles as cliffhangers** | "2016: Free?" · "A Bad Lifeline" · "Attack of the Clones". Use the same titles as YouTube chapters. |
| **Explicit plant** | "This was an early advantage… but as we will get to later, it also eventually became their fatal weakness." |
| **Promise of evidence** | "I'll talk more about it later; I have all these graphs." |
| **Answer the viewer's objection** | "You might be thinking: he promised it would stay independent, so there's nothing to worry about, right? …Right." |
| **Undercut good news** | "'Most improved' doesn't always mean 'great'. Compare anything to a dumpster fire and it'll look good." |
| **Doubt before the confession** | A credible defender ("a respected short seller said it was not a fraud"), just before the truth lands. |
| **Chapter-ending lines** | "But strangely, that didn't matter." · "So what could go wrong?" · "Then Sprint was thrown a lifeline." |

### Reveals
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Withhold the real cause** | Walk through the obvious causes, concede they don't add up ("they were still making tens of millions"), then: "So here's the real issue." |
| **Counterintuitive mini-question** | "Why would a company inflate its *expenses*?" Answer: to explain where the cash went. |
| **Show the investigator's method** | "They hired hundreds of people to film 10,000 hours of store traffic." |
| **Irony punch** | Quote the company's confidence ("You can charge a premium price if you build the best machines."), then: "A few months later, he retired." |
| **Scene with a demonstration** | The chairman's secret team built two PCs in a motel room in three days: "Yes, you can." |
| **The imitator wins** | "The copycat ended up buying the original." Plus an everyday analogy. |
| **Fairness as a plot twist** | A late section, "In defence of…", that steelmans the company. |

### Real voices and posts
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Sound-up clip** | Narrator: "Subway's position was simple." → cut to the CEO on CNBC saying it himself (6s) → narrator reacts. 3–6 per episode, 3–12s each, ~5–10% of runtime. |
| **The real person, not a paraphrase** | If a founder said the key line on camera, use the clip rather than a quote card: it's more credible, and it breaks up the AI voice. |
| **Quote as its source** | Quotes from anywhere (article, interview, book, filing, earnings call, press release, any social platform), shown in the form they came from: a clipping, a transcript, a page, a post card. Reads as evidence, not assertion. |
| **Real logos** | Real brand logos on screen, beside chart bars and in thumbnails, for instant recognition. Identification only, never implied endorsement. |
| **Local-news human** | A franchisee or customer interviewed on local TV gives the human cost in 5 seconds. |

### Voice
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Blunt-answer interrupt** | "So what did Sprint do? They fired them. That's right, fired." |
| **Rule-of-three comic juxtaposition** | "AT&T offered the iPhone. Verizon offered the Droid. Sprint offered… the Palm Pre?" |
| **Everyday analogies for finance** | "Like a person living so far beyond their means that a temporary pay cut led to the bank foreclosing on their house." |
| **Opinions with an edge, labelled** | "The people who it's hardest to feel sorry for: the hosts." |
| **Light first-person verdicts** | "Here's what I think…" at verdict moments only, even on a faceless channel. |

### Endings
| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Return to the title question** | "Let's again return to the original question…", then the answer in one breath. |
| **Bookend** | The opening line comes back with new meaning. |
| **Callback phrase** | "House of cards", planted mid-video, closes the final question. |
| **The twin's answer** | "Sprint had a bad network and missed the iPhone, but that's not why it died. T-Mobile was in the same boat." |
| **What happened next** | The latest outcome, often bigger than the story itself. |
| **The earned subscribe line** | See above. Never "like, subscribe and share". |
