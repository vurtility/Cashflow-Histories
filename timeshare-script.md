# Timeshares: Script

**Working title:** Why You Can't Give a Timeshare Away
**Runtime:** ~[measured] min
**Fact-check status:** checked 13 Nov 2026; see section 11
**Format:** industry paradox ladder told through one founder (David Siegel, Westgate), with the public timeshare companies' filings for the numbers and a worked hypothetical at the midpoint. Beat sheet from `references/formats.md` (A + power story).
**Topic scores (1–5):** reach 4 · hidden-economics gap 5 · contrarian thesis 5 · comparison potential 3 (Westgate, Marriott, Hilton, Wyndham) · data availability 4 (10-Ks, ARDA) · nostalgia 3 · timeliness 3 (Siegel died 2025; maintenance fees jumping) · story material 5 (Versailles, the 2012 email, the 8-hour pitch)
**Earning logic:** real estate, travel and finance advertisers; "how to get out of a timeshare" is a huge evergreen search; everyone has been offered a free gift for a 90-minute presentation.
**What happened next:** David Siegel died on 5 April 2025, aged 89; Versailles was reported close to finished. Average maintenance fees rose to $1,550 in 2025. Checked 13 Nov 2026.

## 1. Research dossier

| # | Item | This episode |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The assumption** | A timeshare is a cheap way to own a holiday home, sold by pushy salespeople. Owners got a bad deal; the companies got lucky. |
| 2 | **The truth** | You don't buy a week of holiday; you pay for the machine that sold it to you. At Marriott Vacations, marketing and sales cost about half of every sale. That's why the resale value collapses the moment you sign: nobody pays for the free gifts and the sales floor twice. Then the company lends you the price at ~14.5%, books an allowance for the one in five dollars it expects never to collect, and keeps the maintenance fee rising forever. |
| 3 | **Central question** | If a timeshare is worth almost nothing the moment you sign, why do people pay $25,000 for one, and how do the companies make billions? |
| 4 | **Final payoff** | The product is the sale. The tour, the loan and the fee are the business; the week of holiday is the wrapping. |

## 7. Script

### COLD OPEN

`[BROLL: eBay-style listing: "Timeshare week · $1"]`
On the internet, you can find timeshares for sale for one dollar. Some owners will even pay the closing costs, just to get rid of them.

`[GFX: "2025: $10.7B of new timeshares · average $24,740"]`
And yet in 2025, Americans bought ten point seven billion dollars of brand-new timeshares, at an average of almost twenty-five thousand dollars each.

`[VISUAL: title card]`
So why would anyone pay twenty-five thousand dollars for something that's worth almost nothing the moment they sign?
◆ **L1 OPENED**

`[PHOTO: Versailles house]`
And how did one man selling them build the biggest house in America?
◆ **L2 OPENED**
[PAUSE 0.8]

### 1. THE ORANGE GROVE

`[BROLL: Orlando, 1980s]`
In 1980, David Siegel owned an orange grove near Orlando. One day, a man offered to buy part of it.

`[SOT 12.0s: David Siegel, Westgate founder — "I said, what are you going to do with it? He says, I'm going to timeshare it. I said, what's that? He explained the concept to me. I fell in love with the concept. I didn't sell him the property. I decided I'm going to do it myself." | Fox Business, 2017]`

`[BROLL: Westgate resort]`
He built sixteen villas at the back of the grove. That became Westgate Resorts, which grew into one of the biggest timeshare companies in the world.

`[BROLL: Versailles house under construction]`
In 2004, he and his wife Jackie started building a house. Ninety thousand square feet. Modelled on the palace of Versailles.

`[SOT 6.1s: Jackie Siegel — "It just became the largest home in America. We weren't trying to build the largest home in America. It just happened." | Fox Business, 2017]`

`[BROLL: ABC News, 2012]`
When the 2008 crash came, construction stopped. Credit dried up, timeshare sales collapsed, and Westgate cut jobs.

`[SOT 4.8s: ABC News / David Siegel — "Her husband is 77-year-old billionaire David Siegel." / "I don't believe in wasting money." | ABC Good Morning America, Jul 2012]`

`[DOC: CNN Money, Oct 2012: Siegel's email to employees]`
In 2012, Siegel emailed his seven thousand employees, warning that new taxes could force him to cut jobs if Barack Obama were re-elected. The email went viral.

`[BROLL: Versailles, construction resumes, 2013]`
Then the business came back. And so did the house.
[PAUSE 0.4]
So how does selling one week a year in Orlando pay for a palace?
◆ **CLIFFHANGER #1**
[PAUSE 0.8]

### 2. THE FREE GIFT

`[BROLL: resort lobby, sales desk]`
It starts with a free gift. Show tickets. A cheap hotel stay. A cruise. All you have to do is sit through a ninety-minute presentation.

`[GFX: "Hilton Grand Vacations, 2025: 856,676 tours · $3,851 sold per tour, on average"]`
The companies call it a tour, and they count them. In 2025, Hilton Grand Vacations ran over eight hundred and fifty thousand of them. On average, every tour brought in three thousand eight hundred and fifty-one dollars of sales.

`[BROLL: KPIX report, 2015]`
That's why the free gift is worth it. And why ninety minutes can turn into something else. In 2015, a couple on holiday in Mexico told a San Francisco TV station what happened to them.

`[SOT 10.2s: KPIX reporter / Susie High, timeshare buyer — "That 90 minutes they say turned into eight excruciating hours of rotating and relentless salespeople pressuring them." / "They kept pushing us and pushing us and we'd say no and they'd say something else." | KPIX 5 News, 24 Apr 2015]`

`[DOC: Florida Statutes §721.10: "10-day right to cancel"]`
It's why many US states give timeshare buyers a few days to cancel, by law. In Florida, it's ten. Most people never use it.
◆ **CLIFFHANGER #2** (what did they actually buy?)
[PAUSE 0.8]

### 3. WHERE THE MONEY GOES

`[GFX: "Marriott Vacations, 2025: contract sales $1.8B"]`
Here's what the companies' own accounts show. In 2025, Marriott Vacations sold about one point eight billion dollars of timeshares.

`[GFX: "Marketing and sales: $943M"]`
It spent nine hundred and forty-three million on marketing and selling them.
[PAUSE 0.4]
About half.

`[GFX: "Of every $1 you pay: ~52¢ to sell it to you"]`
So of every dollar a buyer pays, about fifty cents pays for the free gifts, the tours, the sales floor and the commissions. Only what's left pays for the holiday and the profit.
◆ **MIDPOINT REVEAL (part 1)**

`[BROLL: resale listing]`
Which is why you can't give it away. The day you sign, you're holding half a product. The next buyer won't pay for the free gift you got.

`[SOT 2.8s: World Property Channel guest, NBC Today — "You typically are making 20 cents on a dollar if you're lucky to sell it." | NBC Today, 23 May 2011]`
◆ **L1 CLOSED (part 1)**

`[BROLL: signing paperwork]`
But the sale is only the beginning. Most buyers don't pay twenty-five thousand dollars in cash. The company lends it to them.

`[GFX: "Hilton Grand Vacations: timeshare loans average 14.5% interest"]`
At Hilton Grand Vacations, those loans charge an average of fourteen and a half percent.

`[VISUAL: "Simplified example: an average timeshare"]`
Here's an average timeshare, with simple numbers.

`[GFX: "$24,740 at 14.5% over 10 years = $392 a month"]`
Twenty-four thousand seven hundred and forty dollars, borrowed at fourteen and a half percent over ten years, is about three hundred and ninety-two dollars a month.

`[GFX: "Total paid: ~$47,000"]`
By the end, you've paid about forty-seven thousand dollars.

`[GFX: "Travel + Leisure: 20.7% of sales set aside for loans that won't be repaid"]`
The companies know many buyers won't make it. Travel + Leisure, one of the biggest timeshare sellers, sets aside about twenty-one cents of every dollar it sells, for loans it expects never to collect.
[PAUSE 0.4]
And when a buyer stops paying, the company can take the week back, and sell it again, to the next family on a free trip.
◆ **MIDPOINT REVEAL (part 2)**
[PAUSE 0.8]

### 4. THE FEE THAT NEVER ENDS

`[GFX: "Average maintenance fee: $1,260 (2023) → $1,480 (2024) → $1,550 (2025)"]`
Then there's the part that never ends. Every timeshare comes with a yearly maintenance fee. In 2023, the average was twelve hundred and sixty dollars. By 2025, it was fifteen hundred and fifty.

`[GFX: "Ten years: ~$47,000 loan + $15,500+ fees ≈ $62,500 for ten one-week holidays"]`
Add ten years of fees to the loan, and our average buyer has paid around sixty-two thousand dollars for ten weeks of holiday. About six thousand dollars a week.

`[BROLL: KPIX consumer report, 2017]`
And the fee keeps coming after the loan is paid off. Owners who want out often can't find anyone to take it.

`[SOT 3.7s: Gary, timeshare owner — "Anybody even came in and said, I'll give you 50 cents, I would have said it's yours." | KPIX 5 News, Jul 2017]`

`[DOC: California real estate commissioner warning on timeshare exit companies]`
That desperation has created a second industry: exit companies that promise to get owners out, for an upfront fee. Regulators warn many of them take the money and do nothing.
◆ **CLIFFHANGER #3**
[PAUSE 0.8]

### VERDICT

`[VISUAL: title card]`
So why can't you give a timeshare away?

`[GFX: the money: "Sale ~50% → selling it · loan 14.5% · fee forever"]`
Because you were never buying a holiday. About half of what you paid went on selling it to you. The company then lent you the rest at fourteen and a half percent, and charges you a fee that rises every year, for as long as you own it.

`[PHOTO: Versailles]`
David Siegel died in April 2025, aged eighty-nine. The palace he started in 2004 was nearly finished.

`[BROLL: resort pool]`
He built it on a simple discovery from an orange grove in 1980: the holiday isn't the product. The sale is.

`[END CARD]`
