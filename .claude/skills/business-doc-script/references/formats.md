# Formats

There are seven structures. Pick one in Stage 2 (or combine two). All use the same craft rules (`framework.md`); they differ in what forms the spine.

---

## A. Single-company paradox ladder

**Use when:** one company has a strong gap between what people assume and how its money really works (the hotel giants barely own hotels; WhatsApp made almost nothing for years and still sold for $19B; Costco makes its profit from membership fees).

**Spine:** one central paradox, teased in the cold open and answered at the midpoint, then a threat and a verdict.

```
COLD OPEN  paradox + stakes + promise; L1 (title question) opened; a plant
ACT 1      the setup / origin: a mini-loop opened and closed; cliffhanger
ACT 2      "the obvious answer", told as 2–3 stories with their own mini-loops; ends "…but the maths still doesn't work"
ACT 3      MIDPOINT REVEAL: the strongest derived number answers L1; opens "so why would they…?"; the plant pays off
ACT 4      the threat: stakes rise; twist; cliffhanger
ACT 5      verdict: callback to the cold-open image with new meaning; the future question
```

Worked examples: the WhatsApp and Airbnb-vs-hotels audits in `audit-log.md`.

## B. Industry tournament

**Use when:** the topic is a category with several recognisable brands at different price points or eras (steakhouses, hotels, cookie shops, airlines, gyms, fast food). This is the genre's most reliable format: viewers get a "which one is mine?" reason to keep watching, and per-store numbers for every brand.

**Its usual failure:** it becomes "and then the next brand", a list of profiles, with the title question answered early or never revisited. The fixes:

1. **Ask the tournament question in the cold open**, phrased as a paradox, not a menu. Not "we'll cover Outback, Texas Roadhouse and Ruth's Chris" but "The steakhouse making the most per restaurant isn't the one winning. The cheapest chain on this list is."
2. **Order the contenders so each one answers the previous one's problem.** This turns a list into a chain of "but/therefore". Example: Mrs. Fields (high margins, but tiny sales and exposed to rent) → Insomnia (solves location) → Levain (solves "you can only charge so much for a cookie") → Crumbl (combines every lesson). Each contender's section ends on its weakness, which becomes the next one's opening.
3. **Give every contender the same per-unit scorecard** so viewers can compare: sales per store, average check, cost of goods %, labour, rent, store-level margin, store count over time, and the franchise share of revenue. Show it as a recurring graphic that fills in as the video goes.
4. **Plant a benchmark early and pay it off later.** Example: a dying brand's franchisee economics early on ("after rent, there's nothing left for payroll"), then the reveal that the hot brand's franchisees have sunk to the same level. That callback is the strongest moment of the format.
5. **Hold the verdict.** Individual contenders can have their own reveals, but the tournament question ("which model wins, and why?") stays open until the final act. If a reveal answers the title early, move it later, or follow it with a bigger question.
6. **End on a verdict that reframes the category**, not a summary of each brand. E.g. "The best restaurant business isn't the one with the best margins; it's the one that doesn't run restaurants."

```
COLD OPEN  the most surprising per-unit comparison; the tournament question as a paradox; contenders teased, not listed
ROUND 1    contender A: what it got right, its per-unit numbers, its weakness → cliffhanger
ROUND 2    contender B solves A's weakness… but creates a new one → cliffhanger
ROUND 3    contender C combines the lessons, and wins? → "but when you dig into the numbers…"
REVEAL     the hidden economics (often franchisor vs franchisee, fees vs operations, owned vs managed); callback to the planted benchmark
THREAT     a new entrant or shock that tests every model
VERDICT    which model wins and why; reframe the cold open; the future question
```

**Optional segment:** original reporting (an interview with an operator, franchisee or independent). It works best placed *before* the verdict as evidence, not after it as an epilogue. In one audited script, 17 minutes of independent-shop stories came after the main question had already been answered.

## C. Power story

**Use when:** the best material is about control: who holds power, how they keep it, and what they did when it was threatened. Typical subjects: family conglomerates, succession battles, hostile takeovers, regulatory capture, a company too big for its country. Worked example: a script on Samsung and South Korea.

**Spine:** a paradox about power ("this country should be poor"; "this family owns a few percent of the company but controls all of it"), a mechanism the viewer learns, a threat to that power, then a heist-like sequence where the powerful act to keep it.

```
COLD OPEN  the paradox in the first line, with evidence stacked on it; the company named as the answer, which opens a bigger question
ACT 1      origin: how a small business became powerful; each step "but/therefore"; the stakes of the era (war, crisis)
ACT 2      how big it is now: one striking comparison (with rivals or other countries) plus "you" moments ("when you buy a phone… insurance… a theme-park ticket…")
ACT 3      THE MECHANISM: a worked hypothetical teaches how control works; then "these structures are fragile, especially when…"
ACT 4      THE THREAT + HEIST: a real event makes the risk concrete (interrupt cliffhanger); a plan; opposition; a chain reveal of who was pulled in
ACT 5      CONSEQUENCES + PATTERN: what happened next (close every thread); a parallel from the past; the strongest version of the other side's case
VERDICT    the larger point (who needs whom); callback to an image or phrase from earlier; the future question
```

**Numbers still matter here.** Bring in the derived numbers the story needs (the merger ratio's value transfer, the tax bill against the stake, the share of exports), but the climax is the story turn.

### C2. Fraud exposé (power-story variant)
A fixed six-beat arc that works like a thriller:
1. **The dazzling rise:** speed records, famous investors, a valuation that makes no sense.
2. **The doubter:** a short seller, journalist or employee, *and their method* (the most loved detail).
3. **The denial:** the company's furious rebuttal.
4. **The defender:** a credible voice backs the company, so the viewer wavers.
5. **The confession / proof:** the moment it's undeniable, with the number.
6. **The aftermath:** fines, jail, and the ironic human detail. **Then what happened next.**

## D. Twin/foil

**Use when:** two companies faced the same problem and got opposite outcomes (Sprint vs T-Mobile; Blockbuster vs Netflix; Kmart vs Target).

```
COLD OPEN  the foil paradox: "A had more of everything than B. B thrived. A died. How?"
ACT 1      the shared starting point, and A's advantages, made vivid
ACT 2      A's bets, each ending on a cliffhanger; B appears as the threat
ACT 3      the turning point where their choices diverge (a scene)
ACT 4      A's lifeline or last chance, and why it fails
VERDICT    "It wasn't [the obvious cause], because B had that problem too. It was [the decision]."
```
Natural series: make the twin's video next and link the two.

## E. Stakeholder squeeze

**Use when:** the topic is a market or platform (short-term rentals, food delivery, ticketing, app stores) where every party now has a problem.

Map each stakeholder → what they got at first → what changed → the mechanism (with numbers) → who's to blame. Order them from least sympathetic to most. **Plant one insight that pays off across stakeholders.** Example: the platform markets the "stay with a local host" image because shared stays are harder to regulate. End on the platform: is its business actually at risk, or just its image? Make the title only as strong as the evidence.

## F. Founder origin

**Use when:** a mega-brand's early years contain an audacity moment (Phil Knight inventing a company name mid-meeting).

Beats: the founder's insight (often an analogy, e.g. "Japanese cameras beat German ones, so why not shoes?") → **the audacity moment** → scrappy traction with a number ladder ($8K → $20K → $300K) → **a planted dependency** (they relied on a supplier or partner) → the squeeze → **the conflict scene** (a lawsuit, a betrayal, a split; never skip it) → **the escape route pays off** ("they held all the cards") → a reframe, not "and then they grew".

---

## G. Tragedy (the strength that becomes the weakness)

**Use when:** the thing that made a company win later killed it (Compaq's dealer channel; Kodak's film margins; Nokia's hardware culture).

```
COLD OPEN  staccato number ladder of the rise → "That is when the problems began." → the tragedy stated as a thesis
ACT 1      the growth engines, one of which is explicitly flagged: "…it also eventually became their fatal weakness"
ACT 2      the high: hubris quotes (press praise, CEO confidence) for later irony
ACT 3      the attack: the mechanism that commoditises them (worked hypothetical if needed); irony punches
ACT 4      the climax scene (boardroom, a showdown, a demonstration)
ACT 5      the turnaround… then hubris again (a double fall); the plant pays off
VERDICT    the larger lesson; who captured the value instead
```
Keep it under ~25 minutes. The audited example ran 33, and the last third (the merger aftermath) diffused it.
