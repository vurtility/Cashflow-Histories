Twelve high-performing videos from seven channels, audited against `framework.md` in November 2026. The full audit of each video is in `audit-log.md`. Views and multipliers come from YouTube search results and third-party trackers (OutlierKit etc.), so they're approximate.

## Cross-channel findings

1. **Views follow the topic; craft keeps the viewers.** The best-crafted script (Compaq, 49) had a niche topic and got 1.6M views. The two biggest multipliers (Chuck E. Cheese's ~40×, KFC ~24×) scored 42 and 30. What the big multipliers share: **childhood nostalgia, a news peg, universal reach and a repeatable series title**.
2. **The highest-scoring scripts all run on story as well as numbers:** characters, a conflict scene, a twist (Compaq, WhatsApp, Samsung, Sprint). Numbers-only scripts top out around 40.
3. **The most common failure on every channel is a weak opening:** an industry primer, "what's up guys", a spoken table of contents, or a sponsor read in the first 30 seconds. The second most common is **the best fact buried** (KFC's "80% of stores are abroad" used as a closing caveat; the Modern MBA scripts' best numbers 5–16 minutes in).
4. **The title question gets answered too early or never revisited.** The good scripts explicitly return to it ("let's again return to the original question").
5. **Endings are mostly weak:** summaries, comment-bait lists, like/subscribe/share. Bookends, callbacks, the twin's answer and "what happened next" are the strong patterns.
6. **Accuracy is loose across the genre.** Common problems: revenue vs bookings or profit, self-contradicting statistics, unverified clips and "some believe", titles that overclaim, dated stories with no update. **Rigorous sourcing is a real way to stand out.**
7. **Reusable formats found:** single-company paradox ladder, industry tournament, power story (with fraud exposé), twin/foil, stakeholder squeeze, founder origin, tragedy. See `formats.md`.

## Detail: Modern MBA (numbers-led): what all three did well

- **Familiar category, hidden economics.** Everyone has eaten at a chain steakhouse or stayed in a hotel; nobody knows what the place earns.
- **Per-store unit economics as the payoff.** Every brand gets "the average location grosses $X, nets $Y, margin Z%", with year-over-year change. This is the channel's signature and what viewers come back for.
- **Comparisons that make numbers feel real:** a $90 vs $20 average check; $250K vs $60K in yearly royalties per franchise; 6M Airbnb listings vs 2.5M Marriott+Hilton rooms; store margin falling from 30% to 10% while the franchisor's margin stays above 30%.
- **Contrarian titles** that overturn an assumption: "Fails to Disrupt", "Can't Survive", "Fall of", "Rigged", "Secret", "Really Make Money".
- **One genuinely counterintuitive reveal** when they got it right: "the hotel giants already *are* Airbnb, because Hilton owns under 2% of its rooms"; "Crumbl corporate thrives by squeezing its own franchisees".
- **A plant paid off** (Crumbl): Mrs. Fields' "zombie" franchisee economics set up early, then "the average Crumbl franchisee now takes home as much as a present-day Mrs. Fields."
- **Original reporting** (Crumbl): interviews with independent shops, which nobody else can copy.

## Detail: Modern MBA: what all three did badly

- **Slow openings:** an industry primer instead of a hook ("The restaurant industry is…", "Hotels seem like a simple business…", "Every decade has its dessert trends…"). Their best number arrived 5–16 minutes in.
- **A spoken table of contents** instead of a question ("we'll cover X, then Y, then Z").
- **The title question left implicit, answered too early, or never revisited.** Hotels: the question is asked out loud at 16:07 and answered 14 seconds later. Crumbl: it's effectively answered at ~10:00 of 29:30, and the ending never returns to "can it survive?".
- **"And then" lists of brands** (hotels: 4 minutes of brand portfolios and revenue figures), with flat transitions between contenders.
- **Summary endings** that don't reframe the opening.
- **Loose facts and no sources:**
  - Airbnb's revenue described as total bookings (it's the fees; gross bookings were ~8× larger)
  - "Low season… the end of the school year"
  - a garbled Hyatt founding story
  - a profit figure called "grossed"
  - "fast casual" used for a casual-dining chain
  - "earnings" used to mean revenue
  - internal contradictions (store counts, "3×" ratios)
  - "never-before-seen financials" with no source named
  - motives assigned without evidence
  - editing notes ("[a]", "[ad break]") left in.

## The story-led script (Samsung): what it adds

The highest-scoring script, and it runs on a different engine: story and mechanism rather than per-store numbers.
- **The paradox in the first line**, with evidence stacked on it: "Geographically speaking, South Korea should be poor." Then size, farmland, coal, gas and oil rankings, then "but it's not… a fact that has much to do with a single company." The answer arrives at 1:08 and immediately opens a bigger question.
- **A worked hypothetical** teaches cross-shareholding control with two imaginary $50m affiliates and a 49.9% stake. The viewer learns how it works, so the real deal later makes sense.
- **An interrupt cliffhanger:** "These structures are tenuous, especially when someone dies… this hypothetical started to feel far more concrete in May 2014, due to this." Cut to the heart attack.
- **A heist as the climax:** a lopsided merger ratio; a hedge fund objecting; "but there was one whale"; then a chain reveal (pension fund → ministry → government → President); then "conveniently, they had a back door".
- **A pattern payoff:** the son's conviction and pardon mirror his father's a decade earlier.
- **Fairness:** "this justification isn't that far-fetched… it might be pragmatic", before the final argument.
- **The ending** calls back to "house of cards" and closes on a future question.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - It compares revenue with GDP as if revenue were a share of the economy.
  - It overclaims ("rich because of a single company").
  - It never says what happened to the inheritance tax it called a "death blow". The family paid a record bill of about ₩12tn in instalments and kept control, which is a missed final twist.
  - A conviction is described loosely ("jail… for bribery" when the sentence was suspended and the main charges were different).

**Lesson:** the strongest scripts combine both engines. Numbers make the viewer feel clever; story makes them need to know what happens next.

## Implications built into the skill

- Stage 0 topic scoring (reach, hidden-economics gap, contrarian thesis, comparison potential, data availability).
- The tournament format with fixes for its usual failures (`formats.md`).
- Franchise disclosure documents and per-unit tables as standard research.
- The strongest number in the first 30 seconds; no table of contents; the title question held until the final act.
- Fact-check traps: metric definitions, internal consistency, founding stories, revenue ÷ GDP, overclaimed causes.
- The power-story format; the worked hypothetical, chain reveal and interrupt cliffhanger; closing every story thread.
- No sponsor segments. One earned subscribe line at the end, tied to what the viewer learned.
- (After all 12 audits) Topic factors for nostalgia, timeliness and story material; four more formats (twin/foil, stakeholder squeeze, founder origin, tragedy) plus fraud exposé; the device toolkit in `framework.md`; primary-quote sources, an anecdote bank and a "what happened next" check; fact-check traps for titles that overclaim, unverified allegations, self-contradicting statistics and dated stories.
