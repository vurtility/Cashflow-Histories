### 1. THIRTY-TWO STORES

`[GFX: Bridgeport, Connecticut, 1965. A small storefront: "Pete's Super Submarines"]`
In 1965, Fred DeLuca was seventeen and needed money for college. So he asked a family friend, a nuclear physicist named Peter Buck, for advice.
Buck's advice was a sandwich shop. And a thousand dollars to start it.

`[GFX: sign changes "Pete's Super Submarines" → "Subway"]`
The first shop was called Pete's Super Submarines. It later became Subway.

`[GFX: goal card: "32 stores in 10 years". Counter climbs slowly… 4… 9… 16. Year: 1973]`
DeLuca set a goal: thirty-two stores in ten years.
Eight years in, they had sixteen.
He was going to miss it by half.
◆ **L3 OPENED** (how did this become the biggest chain in America?)

`[GFX: map pin, a KFC nearby. Label: "franchised"]`
But DeLuca had noticed something about the KFC nearby. KFC didn't open its own restaurants. Other people did, with their own money, and paid KFC for the name.

`[GFX: flow diagram. Franchisee pays: store build-out, rent, staff, food → sales come in → "12.5% off the top" arrow to Subway]`
So Subway started franchising. And the deal it settled on is the most important thing in this video.
A franchisee pays to build the store, pays the rent, hires the staff and buys the food. Subway takes a slice of every sale. Today that's eight percent in royalties and four and a half percent for advertising. Twelve and a half percent, off the top.

`[VISUAL: line card: "A cut of SALES. Not profit."]`
Off the top of *sales*. Not profit. Subway gets paid whether the store makes money or not.
Remember that.

Because that one detail would turn Subway into the biggest restaurant chain in America… and then start eating it alive.

### 2. A SUBWAY ON EVERY CORNER

`[GFX: two bars race: Subway vs McDonald's, US store count. 2002: Subway 13,247 · McDonald's 13,099]`
In 2002, Subway passed McDonald's to become the chain with the most restaurants in America. By a hundred and forty-eight stores.
And it didn't stop.

`[GFX: map fills with dots, faster and denser. Peak counter: "27,000+ (2015)"]`
By 2015, there were more than twenty-seven thousand Subways in the United States. Twice as many as McDonald's.

`[GFX: territory map with regional boundaries; small badges labelled "development agent"]`
Part of how it grew so fast was a sales force of development agents. These are local franchise brokers who sign up new owners in their territory, find sites and train them. Historically, they earned a share of the fees and the royalties from their area.
◆ **L4 OPENED** — so what happens when there's a Subway on every corner?

`[VISUAL: label "Simplified example"]`
Here's a simple example. Say a town has one Subway, and it sells six hundred thousand dollars of sandwiches a year.

`[GFX: Store A: $600K. Subway's cut: $75K]`
Subway's twelve and a half percent is seventy-five thousand dollars.

`[GFX: Store B pops up two blocks away. A: $450K · B: $450K · combined $900K]`
Now a second Subway opens two blocks away. Some customers are new, but a lot just switch stores. Together they sell nine hundred thousand dollars, but each one only sells four hundred and fifty.

`[GFX: Subway's cut rises $75K → $112.5K (green). Each owner's sales fall $600K → $450K (red)]`
Subway's cut goes up by half, to a hundred and twelve thousand. Both owners are worse off than the first one used to be.
For the company, more stores almost always means more money. For the people running them, it can mean the opposite.

`[QUOTE: "You just end up cannibalizing existing stores." — a Subway franchise owner]`

`[GFX: contract clause highlighted: "reserves the right to open… next door" (paraphrase)]`

`[VISUAL: music lifts]`
But here's the thing about franchisees. Sometimes, they're the ones who save you.

### 4. TWO A DAY

`[GFX: descending line: 27,000+ (2015) → 18,773 (2025). Label: "≈ 8,300 fewer"]`
Ten straight years of shrinking. About eight thousand three hundred fewer Subways than at the peak. Seven hundred and twenty-nine in 2025 alone.

`[GFX: three bars: McDonald's ~$4.09M · Jersey Mike's ~$1.37M · Subway ~$500K. Small print: "Subway: Circana estimate; Subway doesn't disclose"]`
Here's why so many close. The average McDonald's in America sells about four million dollars a year. The average Jersey Mike's, about one point four million.
The average Subway? Around five hundred thousand.

`[GFX: $500K bar; a slice peels off: "12.5% → $62,500". Remaining: food, wages, rent…]`
Take twelve and a half percent off the top, and that's sixty-two thousand five hundred dollars a year before the owner has paid for a single slice of bread, a single wage or a single month's rent.

`[GFX: August 2024 card: "$6.99 FOOTLONG"]`
Then, in 2024, corporate tried to win customers back with a six-ninety-nine footlong.
The franchisees' association told its members to opt out. Most older contracts allowed that. Newer franchisees couldn't.
One owner said the deal made up about a fifth of his sales, with no extra customers to show for it.
◆ **P2 PAID OFF** — *the people who once saved Subway with a deal are now being squeezed by one*

`[QUOTE: Subway: "…the greatest benefit to the entire Subway system"]`
Subway's position was that it has to make decisions for the good of the whole system. It calls the closures "rightsizing": moving stores to locations where franchisees can succeed long-term.

`[GFX: bankruptcy stamps: "River Sub · 48 stores · 2024" · "MTF Enterprises · 43 stores · 2026"]`
But some of the biggest operators haven't made it. One franchisee with forty-eight stores went bankrupt in 2024. Another with forty-three filed this year.

`[VISUAL: music drops]`
So Subway must be in serious trouble. Fewer stores. Falling royalties. Franchisees going under.
◆ **L5 OPENED** — so where's the money going?

Except it isn't.

### 5. HERE'S THE REAL ISSUE

`[GFX: document cover: "Subway Franchise Disclosure Document · April 2026"]`
Every year, Subway has to publish a franchise disclosure document for anyone thinking of buying a franchise. Inside are the company's own accounts.

`[GFX: three bars rise: 2023 $15M · 2024 $397M · 2025 $688M. Label: "Net income"]`
In 2023, Subway's net income was fifteen million dollars. In 2024, three hundred and ninety-seven million. In 2025, six hundred and eighty-eight million.
About forty-six times more profit in two years.

`[GFX: beside it, a smaller red arrow: "Franchise revenue: $818M → $767M (−6%)"]`
Over the same period, Subway's franchise revenue went *down*. Fewer stores paying royalties.
◆ **L5 CLOSED** — *the money was going to corporate*

`[GFX: label "Filing: lower operating expenses"]`
The filing puts the jump down to lower operating expenses. Subway corporate got a lot cheaper to run.

`[VISUAL: callback card: "A cut of SALES. Not profit."]`
Remember the deal from 1974? Subway takes its slice off the top of every sale, from every store.
So when a weak store closes, Subway loses a small royalty. The franchisee loses everything they put in.
◆ **P1 PAID OFF**

`[GFX: "Aug 2023: Roark Capital agrees to buy Subway · up to $9.55B" → stamp "Completed 2024"]`
And there's a reason the company got leaner when it did. In 2023, the DeLuca family and Peter Buck's foundation agreed to sell Subway to the private equity firm Roark Capital for up to nine point five five billion dollars.
The founding families got paid. Roark got a machine that takes a slice of tens of thousands of stores' sales. The people paying for the closures are the franchisees.

`[VISUAL: line card: "Subway isn't dying. Its restaurants are."]`
Subway isn't dying. Its restaurants are.

### 6. THE OTHER SEVENTEEN-YEAR-OLD

`[GFX: Point Pleasant, New Jersey, 1975. Storefront: "Mike's Subs"]`
Which brings us back to the other seventeen-year-old.

`[GFX: a football and a loan document. "$125,000 · from his old football coach (a banker)"]`
In 1975, a high-school senior named Peter Cancro wanted to buy the sub shop where he worked. He was too young to legally use the meat slicer. His old football coach, who happened to be a banker, backed a loan for a hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars.
◆ **L2 CLOSED**

`[GFX: slow store-count line: 1975 → ~3,400 (2026)]`
That shop became Jersey Mike's. And it grew slowly. Fifty years later, it has about three thousand four hundred stores, roughly a sixth of Subway's American footprint.

`[GFX: the per-store bars again: Jersey Mike's ~$1.37M vs Subway ~$500K]`
But each one sells nearly three times as much as the average Subway.

`[GFX: July 2026: "NYSE: JMKE · IPO at $23 · ≈ $7.3B"]`
In July this year, Jersey Mike's went public at a value of about seven point three billion dollars.

`[GFX: price-per-store comparison. Subway: $9.55B ÷ ~37,000 stores ≈ $260K. Jersey Mike's: $7.3B ÷ ~3,400 ≈ $2.2M. Small print: "Different years and deal types; approximate"]`
Divide what each company was valued at by its number of stores. Each Subway restaurant was valued at around two hundred and sixty thousand dollars. Each Jersey Mike's at over two million.
Roughly eight times as much.

### VERDICT

`[VISUAL: return to the cold-open counter: "2 a day"]`
So let's go back to the question. How does Subway close two restaurants a day and make more money than it has in years?

Because the thing Subway sells was never really sandwiches. It sells the right to sell its sandwiches, and it takes twelve and a half percent of every one.
That model built the biggest restaurant chain in America, one store at a time, and often one store too close to the last. And it means Subway's corporate profits and its franchisees' livelihoods can move in completely opposite directions.

`[GFX: Subway vs Jersey Mike's side by side: "more stores" vs "better stores"]`
Two seventeen-year-olds. One built the most stores. The other built better ones. Fifty years later, the market has decided which was worth more.

`[GFX: "2026: ~100 new US Subways planned" · "China: 4,000-store deal"]`
Subway's new owners plan to open only about a hundred new American stores this year, while signing a deal for four thousand in China. Which leaves one question: can a chain shrink its way back to health? And if it can, who pays for it?
